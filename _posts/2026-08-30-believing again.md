There’s something strangely comforting about self-help books in that they often start from the assumption that the world is good after all; by design, even. It’s just that you’ve forgotten it. 

I’ve read more than my fair share of books on creativity over the years (hello, Julia Cameron, morning pages et al.) and gone down the academic rabbit hole with Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi’s _Creativity_, artists waging war on resistance in Steven Pressfield’s _The War of Art_, the subtle series of decisions that add up to creative output in David Lynch’s _Catching the Big Fish_, and so many more. 

This book echoes many of the lessons of others, such as dealing effectively with fear, perfectionism, etc. But for all its Finding Your North Star, Feel the Fear vibes, Elizabeth Gilbert’s _Big Magic_ is surprisingly full of what feels like applicable common sense. Like…

- **Don’t quit your day job!** Don’t force your art to provide a living for you. Gilbert is pretty emphatic about that: “I have watched many people murder their creativity by demanding that art pay their bills… you can always make your art on the side of your bread-and-butter job… Unless you come from landed gentry, that’s what everyone does.”

- **Follow your curiosity.** “Passion can seem intimidatingly out of reach at times – a distant tower of flame, accessible only to genuises and to those who are specially touched by God. But curiosity is a milder, quieter, more welcoming, and more democratic entity… Curiousity only ever asks one simple question: ‘Is there anything you’re interested in?’” Gilbert urges people to follow their tiny threads of interest in the spirit of a ‘scavenger hunt’, trusting that it may lead somewhere… or nowhere. But the outcome doesn’t matter: for better to have spent your time being inquisitive and living a rich life than the alternative.

- **Creativity for its own sake.** “I firmly believe that we all need to find something to do in our lives that stops us from eating the couch. Whether we make a profession out of it or not, we all need an activity that is beyond the mundane and take us out of our established and limiting roles in society (mother, employee, neighbour, brother, boss, etc)… Prayer can do that for us, community service can do it, sex can do it, exercise can do it, and substance abuse can most certainly do it (albeit with god-awful consequences) – but creative living can do it too. Perhaps creativity’s greatest mercy is this: By completely absorbing our attention for a short and magical spell, it can relieve us temporarily of the dreadful burden of being who we are.” (p172)
    
- **Choose done over perfect.** Perfection is unachievable and can stop you from even starting in the first place. Gilbert channels writer Rebecca Solnit: “So many of us believe in perfection, which ruins everything else, because the perfect is not only the enemy of the good; it’s also the enemy of the realistic, the possible, and the fun.” (p166)
    
- **Lastly, give yourself permission.** Making stuff means you believe you’re allowed to have a voice, you’re allowed to be here. Poet David Whyte calls this “the arrogance of belonging”, which as Gilbert points out, isn’t about egotism or self-absorption: “In a strange way, it’s actually the opposite; it is a divine force that will actually take you out of yourself and allow you to engage more fully with life.” (p93)
